Cordelia

Keep the wolves away from the door, and remember for them so the doctor doesn't have to guess.

The phone

Plugs in between the wall jack and the phone they already have. Same number. If the box ever loses power, the phone works exactly as before — that's wired in, not promised.

Who's calling?

People on the family's list ring straight through, name spoken first. Everyone else is asked to say who they are. No "detecting scams" — we verify the real and let the rest wait.

The evening call

One ring at the time they choose. "How's tonight, one to ten?" Press a number. "Where?" Press a number. Under thirty seconds. Logged on the box, nowhere else.

One line to family

Every night: "Checked in. Two unknown calls, both declined." Never the details. No text by nine means someone calls Mom — which is the product working.

For the doctor

One button prints a dated one-page body map and timeline from their own words — the same clipboard the clinic uses, filled in at home over months.

The fridge card

Three rules and a family word — the part that works even without the box. Free. We give it away.

The Cordelia Promise

Not a policy. A structural fact about how the box is built — and the reason a family picks us over a company that wants their data.

Your information stays in your house.

We don't collect it. We never see it. We never sell it. Unplug it and it's gone.

  • Everything — the call list, the check-ins, the family's numbers — lives on the box in the house. No Vallhund server holds a copy. There is nothing to breach, nothing to subpoena, nothing an investor can ask us to sell.
  • The only thing that leaves the house is the nightly line to family, encrypted end to end. The family holds the keys; we can't read it.
  • The box never knows a name. Updates are signed; only we can push code, and updates go in — nothing comes out.
  • No remote access without someone in the house pressing a button.
  • It works after we're gone. No cloud means the box keeps working even if this company disappears tomorrow.
  • Provable, not promised: the box's code will be open, and the plain-English threat model published.

Selling or sharing customer data is not a part of this business that we are choosing to forgo. It is not a part of this business at all. If the company fails because of that, fine.

How we build

Five rules, carried from the first product to the last.

  • Verify the real; don't detect the fake. The only thing that beats an AI voice clone is knowing who's actually on the list.
  • Fail safe in hardware. If our part dies, their part keeps working. Wired that way, not promised.
  • The smallest, least threatening body that can do the job. A box before a screen. A phone call before a tablet. A rope before a radio. A small dog before anything with a face.
  • Control stays with the person. Taught by demonstration, held by a leash, told "protocol says" — never handed to a cloud.
  • AI at the back, never at the front. We use it to write the rules at our desk. It never touches a customer's data, because there is none to touch.

About

Vallhund Cybernetics is a one-person, veteran-owned company in Fayetteville, North Carolina. The founder served as an Army combat medic and then in Special Operations Civil Affairs — the medic on a four-man team — and ran his unit's unmanned-aircraft program before retiring. He builds what's on this page himself: the code, the fabrication, the specs. The company runs on its own AI operating layer — one person, with an AI staff that drafts, checks, and tracks — which is how a one-person shop turns a spec around in days, not months. The AI stays at the desk; it never touches a customer's data or a customer's box.

The name is a small Swedish herding dog: short legs, long back, bred to work close to the ground and keep the herd together. Cordelia is the shepherd moon that keeps a planet's ring in place — and Lear's daughter, the one who stayed.

Company: a trade name of KEMET Holdings LLC, a service-disabled veteran-owned small business.
